## Zettelkasten

**"Introduction to the Zettelkasten Method"** — Sascha Fast, Zettelkasten.de, 2020 —
<https://zettelkasten.de/introduction/>

The foundational reference for the Luhmann slip-box method, reproducing the core
argument from Luhmann's 1981 essay "Communicating with Slip Boxes." The slip-box is
not a storage device but a thinking partner — the productive unit is not the note but
the *web of notes*, generated by atomicity and explicit links. AXI25 takes both
principles wholesale into `20-wiki/`: one idea per file, minimum 3 connections per
page.

---

**"How to Take Smart Notes"** — Sönke Ahrens, 2017 (2nd ed. 2022) —
<https://www.soenkeahrens.de/en/takesmartnotes>

The accessible modern treatment of the Zettelkasten for contemporary knowledge work.
Introduces the three-tier model: fleeting notes (raw capture) → literature notes
(processed source material) → permanent notes (your own synthesis, in your own words).
Central argument: most note-taking systems fail because they optimize for input, not
output; writing is thinking, and the slip-box forces writing. AXI25 maps the three
tiers directly: `00-capture/` → `25-studies/` (or `35-worklogs/`) → `20-wiki/`.

## PARA & Building a Second Brain

**"Building a Second Brain: The Definitive Introductory Guide"** — Tiago Forte, Forte
Labs, 2023 — <https://fortelabs.com/blog/basboverview/>

Forte's central claim: *"our brains are for having ideas, not storing them."* The PARA
method organizes everything by actionability — **P**rojects, **A**reas, **R**esources,
**A**rchive — rather than by topic. The CODE pipeline describes the full loop from
input to output: **C**apture, **O**rganize, **D**istill, **E**xpress. Progressive
Summarization is the distillation technique: returning to notes across multiple passes,
each time highlighting only what still matters. AXI25 borrows the actionability lens
and the capture-to-distill pipeline; it replaces manual progressive summarization with
the AI-operated Deep Extraction Standard ([Techniques](03-techniques.md#deep-extraction-standard-des)).

## Evergreen notes & digital gardens

**"Evergreen notes"** — Andy Matuschak —
<https://notes.andymatuschak.org/z5E5QawiXCMbtNtupvxeoEX>

Matuschak's definition of notes built to evolve and accumulate across projects, not
just capture in the moment. Three required properties: atomic (one concept per note),
concept-oriented (titled as a claim, not a source), and densely linked. The key
observation: most notes people take are transient, and that's fine — the discipline
is in identifying the few that deserve to become evergreen. This transient-vs-evergreen
distinction is what AXI25 formalizes as the maturity ladder.

---

**"A Brief History & Ethos of the Digital Garden"** — Maggie Appleton, 2020 —
<https://maggieappleton.com/garden-history>

Traces the digital garden concept from Mike Caulfield's 2015 essay "The Garden and
the Stream": the garden (a cultivated, linked space of ideas) vs. the stream (a
chronological feed of posts). Appleton surveys how the garden ethos — public, linked,
non-linear, always in progress — emerged as a reaction to the performative finality of
blogging. Useful background for understanding why AXI25 is organized as an always-
evolving wiki rather than a collection of finished documents.

---

**Linking Your Thinking (LYT) / Maps of Content** — Nick Milo —
<https://www.linkingyourthinking.com/>

Milo's system introduces Maps of Content (MOCs) as flexible index notes — navigational
hubs that sit above atomic notes without imposing rigid hierarchy. MOCs replace the
folder-or-tag dichotomy with a third option: an evolving, curated index that grows with
your knowledge. AXI25 ships MOCs as `20-wiki/maps/`. See
[Techniques](03-techniques.md#maps-of-content-mocs) for how to use them.

## The LLM Wiki pattern

**"LLM Wiki"** — Andrej Karpathy, GitHub Gist, 2026 —
<https://gist.github.com/karpathy/442a6bf555914893e9891c11519de94f>

Karpathy's design note for a pattern where an LLM *incrementally builds and maintains*
a persistent, interlinked Markdown wiki as you feed it sources — rather than
re-deriving knowledge from raw chunks at query time (standard RAG). His framing: the
knowledge is *"compiled once and then kept current, not re-derived on every query,"*
and the wiki becomes *"a persistent, compounding artifact"* where *"the cross-references
are already there."* His diagnosis of why humans abandon wikis — the maintenance burden
grows faster than the value, and LLMs don't get bored, don't forget to update a
cross-reference, and can touch 15 files in one pass — is the core motivation for
AXI25.

**Accuracy note.** "LLM Wiki" is Karpathy's term for this 2026 design note; "wiki-llm"
is community shorthand derived from the gist filename. AXI25 is *inspired by* the
pattern — not affiliated with Karpathy or his work. Do not conflate with his separate
"Software 2.0" (2017 essay) or "LLM OS" framings; those are distinct ideas about
different things.

---

**(context) "As We May Think"** — Vannevar Bush, *The Atlantic*, 1945 —
<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memex>

Bush's 1945 essay imagined the Memex: a desk-sized device for storing and retrieving
documents via associative trails rather than hierarchical indexes. The Memex is the
intellectual ancestor of hypertext, wikis, and the LLM Wiki pattern — the idea that
knowledge should be navigable by association, not by alphabetical filing, predates
the computer by years. Cited here for historical context, not as a direct method
source.

## Zeitgeist

**"Zeitgeist"** — Wikipedia —
<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeitgeist>

Zeitgeist (German: *Zeit* = time, *Geist* = spirit) means the "spirit of the age" —
the dominant ideas, moods, and assumptions of a period, experienced by participants
as simply how things are. The philosopher Johann Gottfried Herder coined the German
compound in 1769. The concept became central to Hegel's philosophy of history: "no man
can surpass his own time, for the spirit of his time is also his own spirit." AXI25
uses the term for its `50-zeitgeist/` layer: temporal discourse snapshots that capture
what is alive in public conversation right now, with the explicit understanding that
those signals will decay in months. See [The Five Pillars](02-the-five-pillars.md)
§ Zeitgeist for what goes there and what doesn't.

## Note maturity

There is no single definitive source for the concept of note maturity — it emerges
from the convergence of two independent traditions:

The **Zettelkasten three-tier model** (Ahrens 2017, zettelkasten.de) distinguishes
fleeting notes (transient, context-dependent, meant to be processed) from literature
notes (processed source material in your own words) from permanent notes (your synthesis,
meant to last and accumulate). Not every note is meant to be permanent — only ideas
that have been worked earn permanence.

The **BASB Progressive Summarization** technique (Forte, fortelabs.com) arrives at the
same structural conclusion from a different direction: you return to notes across
multiple passes, distilling only what still resonates at each pass. Ideas that don't
survive the passes don't get promoted. The process itself is the filter.

Both frameworks independently arrive at the same truth: **not every thought should
become permanent knowledge immediately.** AXI25 makes this literal with its maturity
ladder — see [Philosophy](01-philosophy.md) for the full argument and the diagram.
